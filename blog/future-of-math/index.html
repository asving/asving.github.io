Beyond progress as proof

September 2026

Draft shared for feedback; not yet listed on the blog.

I have considered myself a mathematician almost as long as I can remember. Mathematics felt like a door into reality that was the free communal property of humanity and yet accessible only to those few who choose to make it their home. For the thirteen years that my life centered almost entirely around mathematics, it did feel like home. My PhD, finishing in early 2023, capped the end of this period and went exceptionally well. I had learned an enormous amount, proved a few pretty things, established a minor reputation within my field and had been offered postdoctoral positions at prestigious institutions for four years. And yet, a few months later, I decided to quit thinking about number theory and pivot to thinking about cognition as a response to the stunning success of the language modeling paradigm in AI. I want to explain why I made this switch and what I learned about the nature of intelligence in the intervening three years. Most of all, I want to think through how my experience might suggest new, and very different, futures for mathematics.

I had a strong sense in 2023 that within two or three years, the descendants of ChatGPT would be better at many aspects of mathematics than me and every other mathematician in the world. This was very much not my view until that point in time and taking this prediction seriously forced me to re-evaluate many fixed beliefs. It felt important to understand how our collective understanding of cognition could miss the language model revolution so badly. Even if my only interest was mathematics, understanding how language models conceived of mathematics promised a deeper understanding of mathematics itself. Coupled with the fundamental uncertainty of the effects of such transformative change on human society, I felt a strong drive directing my intellectual curiosity towards questions about the nature of intelligence.

Three years after my switch, my predictions about the mathematical capabilities of language models seem to have panned out very well. Numerous long-standing open conjectures have been proven by language models in the past few months and many more fall every day. The models are far from perfect, or even far from dominating all human mathematicians intellectually, but it is clear that language models are superhuman at some aspects of mathematics compared to humans. However, the models process mathematics in a fundamentally different way and have different strengths today. The typical proof discovered by a model, even the resolution of a long-standing open problem like the unit-distance conjecture, feels like a (very) clever combination of existing ideas rather than the discovery of a bold new idea. To use an imperfect analogy, the superhuman performance of language models today on mathematics feels more like Deep Blue than AlphaZero.

Why are language models able to prove difficult theorems?

Chess engines were better than humans in the mid 2000s using a combination of hardcoded heuristics amplified by large amounts of computation. While they beat humans quite handily most of the time, there were specific positions that humans could outplay the machine in due to its shortsightedness. More importantly, the way machines played felt 'artificial' to humans and we found it hard to learn conceptual principles from machine games. Contrast this with AlphaZero: this was a neural network trained through self-play to play Go and chess at superhuman levels. Its performance was initially not much better than the earlier generation of chess engines but its play felt very different. It wasn't hard for humans to see the beauty in many moves these machines made, and it felt much easier to learn from AlphaZero-related chess engines compared to the earlier era. However imprecise the feeling may be, there was a strong sense that AlphaZero had good taste in a way that earlier chess engines did not. Language models in some sense are much closer to the AlphaZero paradigm than the earlier search-based chess engines, and yet there have been few or no 'move 37's in math — ideas generated by a language model far outside the current conceptual understanding of mathematicians that are nevertheless deeply generative once understood.

More than a year ago in July 2025 (On the Mechanical Creation of Mathematical Concepts), I speculated that near future language models might be superhuman at mathematics in precisely this way. I drew a distinction between search as a way of exploring a fixed conceptual space and a different, more fundamental process of concept creation. In 'Computational Platonism', I present the activity of mathematics as a cycle that begins with some syntactic exploration (of computations or geometric drawings or even proofs) followed by a phase of integrating this into a semantic understanding: the intuitive, illegible outcome of integrating syntactic traces into a new perspective of the subject domain. This is followed by exploration within the semantic space guiding further production of syntactic exploration (proving conjectures, carrying out more computations or even new definitions embodying the learned semantic intuition) which feeds back into learned semantics.

With this terminology, we might hypothesize that language models create semantic concepts through gradient descent over the course of pretraining and reinforcement learning. This gives them an initial frame to explore mathematics by, and when asked to solve mathematical questions during inference, they leverage their existing concepts to search by deploying their greater computational resources. Currently, they seem to be relatively bad at creating new concepts during inference time based on information present in their context but not integrated during training (such as computations they might have performed on the fly).

Even if language models are never able to coherently create new conceptualizations during inference the way a human can, the training time is so short now that we should expect large jumps in capabilities every few months. Importantly, if this view is correct, we should expect language models to encode superhuman conceptualizations of mathematics as they train more on the products of mathematics being generated currently. As such, we should soon expect them to produce proofs that contain genuinely new insights into the structure of mathematics, even if they are not there right now. Nevertheless, there still remains an open question as to whether the language models will be able to make explicit their implicit understanding of mathematics by creating new definitions and theories, and whether they will be able to build new semantic understanding during inference time - both activities that the best human mathematicians currently do better than language models.

Impact on the mathematical community

However, even the current state of affairs has caused massive tremors to the academic system with many questions around how best to reorganize human mathematical activity and how to integrate language models into mathematical research. Rather vexingly, the answers to these questions almost certainly rest on understanding how the capabilities of language models might improve in the short-term future. Where does this leave mathematics and (human) mathematicians? I see this question as a miniature version of the fundamental question of 'AI alignment': how can we integrate artificial intelligence into human society in a way that maximally increases the flourishing of society as a whole?

'AI alignment' usually refers to the process of embodying human values within AI systems. However, the dual question of finding a place for humans within a world changed by AI seemed to me to be just as important a question, and something the mathematical community is struggling with right now. In September 2026, twenty-five Fields Medalists published a declaration titled 'A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics', objecting to the treatment of open problems as benchmarks and use the term 'misalignment' to refer to the problem of finding a place for human mathematicians in the current post AI world. Mathematics is a small society in numbers, but a vital part of how we conceptualize the world and our relationship to it today. As such, a successful integration of artificial intelligence into the human relation towards mathematics will provide a strong start for the broader question of how we integrate artificial intelligence into the relationship we collectively have with the world. What might mathematics look like once we have digested the presence of artificial intelligence and its ability to do mathematics?

The declaration's authors, and many others, have argued that the end goal of mathematics was never the production of proofs but rather the cultivation of mathematical understanding shaped by the human struggle with mathematics. If language models can do so much of the 'production' of mathematics, and if what we really care about is cultivating good mathematicians, then language models seem like a net loss to society. Correspondingly, some mathematicians have even called for a moratorium on the use of artificial intelligence to prove novel theorems. While I have some sympathy with this view, it seems to be too narrow a vision of what mathematics is. While I was drawn to mathematics because of how it changed me and my conceptualizations about mathematics, I was equally drawn to mathematics because it offered the deepest insights about reality independent of whether I understood them or not. As a scientist, I value and welcome new ways to understand the world independent of who produced such understanding, and independent of whether I can understand them at this current moment in time. In the limit, it is not even important that a human originated said understanding as long as I can eventually integrate it into my perception of the world.

But what does it really mean to say that humans can integrate new conceptual frameworks? It might help to look at historical parallels. Almost two millennia ago, Diophantus posed several arithmetic equations whose successful solution would help cultivate mathematical understanding of numbers. For a long time, before and after the Greeks, such computational problems were touchstones against which mathematicians could sharpen and improve their understanding. However, as we gained greater facility with such computations and especially with the automation of computations in the twentieth century, mathematicians moved towards giving proofs a more central place in order to explain the volume of computations they were generating.

Proofs can be seen as organizing principles for large swaths of computation all at once. By distilling the unifying essence of large families of computations, they help structure the mathematical landscape. The development of the axiomatic method in geometry initially, followed by the rest of mathematics, can be seen as a study of the syntactic structure of proof itself. Indeed, there is a direct line connecting Hilbert's search for a satisfactory understanding of the axiomatic method, Gödel's dashing of these hopes and Turing's formalization of the notion of computation. Proofs and computations are inherently linked as has become clear over the last century. The field of computational complexity has led to many surprising discoveries connecting together computation, provability, randomness and much more.

New mathematics as a consequence of language models

The importance of proofs to mathematics led to the development of a theory of formalization, axiomatics and computation, a theory that has had ramifications far outside of its initial domain. I think we are due for another seminal step forward in this journey. Finding a proof is, or will be, largely automated shortly. This does not mean that all theorems will be proven, much as not all computations have been trivialized. Nevertheless, we can now step back and look at the landscape of proof much as mathematicians in the nineteenth century stepped back and looked at the landscape of computation.

The declaration by Fields Medalists referenced above argues that 'solving problems is only a tool and proxy for achieving the primary goal of conceptual understanding and insight.' That conceptual understanding is what I have been calling semantics. I too believe that it is crucial to the process of mathematics, and precisely for this reason we should aim to make it a first-class citizen of the mathematical universe. Historically, integration has meant building a theory one level up: proofs organized the flood of computations, and we now need something to organize the flood of proofs and the intuitions behind them.

Can we say useful things about the process by which semantics (as the illegible locus of intuition) arises from syntax? At its most prosaic, a language model that can resolve Millennium Prize problems is a (very) complicated mathematical function that implicitly encodes the essence of the mathematics it can prove. One way we can access this implicit knowledge is through sampling proofs by running the language model on a wide variety of mathematical questions, and then trying to reverse-engineer its semantic understanding from these proofs. All ways of transmitting human understanding have been close variants of this process so far, necessarily passing through syntactic traces. But might things be different for language models?

Can we extract semantic understanding directly from the function that the language model is? This is what the field of interpretability tries to do for language models in general, without any specific focus on mathematics, and it is very much in its infancy. We understand very little about how such language should be extracted or in what shape it should be represented. People talk loosely about 'representations' without any precise definition of what they are, or how they are supposed to relate to the 'understanding' encoded by a language model. It is a field calling for a formalization of 'semantics' similar to how the axiomatic method, and especially the notion of computation, formalized the syntactic nature of proof.

Syntax and semantics

What seems to be missing is a more formal calculus of 'intuition'. While the theory of computation formalizes specific kinds of legible reasoning well, 'intuition' seems to have characteristics that make it hard to apply the language of Turing machines to it. I explore this difference more in 'Reasoning Was Not Made for Deduction', but here I would like to share an initial framework for what such a theory might look like. If we conceive of a 'proof' or 'computation' as a path traversed in some syntactic space, we might think of intuition as a much more local notion. Our intuitions tell us what next steps seem promising without necessarily having the end in sight. They resemble much more a 'vector field' in the syntactic space, with the geometry of this vector field implicitly defining the structure of a semantic space. A natural way of formalizing this might be to define the distance between statements A and B as $$d(A,B) = -\log \mathbb{P}_{\text{model}}(\text{a proof of } A \text{ from } B \mid \text{context}).$$ This would assign to any fixed proof a log-probability and different intuitions/models would assign different distances relative to their own semantic space. An early human example might be Descartes' unification of algebra and geometry. Indeed, if we identify the points of syntactic space with mathematical statements (in a fixed language) and paths with proofs of implications from one statement to another, then a language model trained in this format gives rise to a 'probabilistic vector field' over this space quite literally, with its output probabilities pointing at the different paths one could take between statements.

Intelligence perhaps has more to do with constructing and exploring such semantic spaces, rather than proving any particular set of results. Understanding the shape of these spaces seems to be of the utmost importance for extracting insight from automated provers like language models. For instance, we should be able to compare intuition trained on Euclidean geometry and intuition trained on Diophantine arithmetic and extract the similarities between them mirroring Descartes' invention of coordinate geometry. We should be able to merge, combine and fine-tune intuitions based on the corresponding geometry. We should be able to explain ahead of time what kinds of proofs are likely for a language model to achieve or not from this extracted geometry, ahead of actually sampling the model. Conversely, we should be able to predict the shape of the intuition from the syntax it is trained on. Most of all, we should be able to transform the intuition into explicit definitions, conjectures and heuristics that make the intuition easier to express.

The value of mathematics

Where does all of this leave mathematics? The Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton is perhaps the innermost sanctum of modern mathematics. Its permanent faculty are an exclusive and eminent class of mathematicians whose tastes often decide what is fashionable within mathematics. When I visited for the 2024-25 academic year, however, Akshay Venkatesh and Govind Menon ran a seminar looking back at older mathematicians, going all the way back to the Ancient Greeks. The explicit goal of the seminar was to learn from how previous generations of mathematicians had reacted to decisive changes in how mathematics was practiced and perceived. We wanted to learn what the past had to teach us about the present moment in time.

What I took away from the seminar was a much more expansive view of what mathematics had been, and could be. Reading back through history certainly made the modern focus on proofs and axiomatics seem overly narrow. Mathematics, to my reading, has always been about finding the right language in which to express our understandings of fundamental notions such as space, time and the nature of the mind. Until very recently, mathematics has been an integral part of the scientific world view, taking as much inspiration as it gave. Discoveries about the motion of planets motivated the invention of calculus, one of the central tools of modern mathematics. Gauss tried to measure the curvature of space in order to check whether we lived in a non-Euclidean world. A long history of wondering about the nature of our mind and the nature of logical operations culminated in the discovery of computers and the theory of computation.

The post-war period of narrow, specialized mathematics is an aberration in the long view and the current moment in time might be forcing us back to a re-examination of why we do mathematics at all. Ultimately, my deepest reason for doing mathematics is connected with understanding the deepest questions about reality. We now have a chance to understand the nature of mind in much more precise detail, mostly powered by mathematics — neural networks are deeply downstream of the mathematical tradition! My proposal of building a 'calculus of intuition' is just one attempt at an effective theory of why deep learning works. Even if it is on the right track, it is only going to be one part of the puzzle and the consequences of such investigations will probably completely reshape our understanding in entirely unexpected ways, as every past moment of scientific progress has done. This understanding is almost certainly a necessary step towards finding the correct place for human mathematicians in the new society we are building. It would be a pity if mathematicians left the most important scientific question of our time to others.
